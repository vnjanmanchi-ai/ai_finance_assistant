# What Is an ETF (Exchange-Traded Fund)?

An exchange-traded fund, or ETF, is a basket of securities — stocks, bonds, or other assets — bundled into a single investment that trades on an exchange just like an individual stock. Buying one share of an ETF gives you proportional exposure to everything the fund holds.

## Why ETFs became popular

ETFs offer instant diversification without needing to buy dozens of individual securities yourself. A single S&P 500 ETF, for example, gives an investor exposure to 500 of the largest U.S. companies in one purchase, spreading risk across many companies and sectors rather than concentrating it in one.

## ETFs vs. mutual funds

Both pool investor money into a diversified portfolio, but they differ in a few practical ways:

- **Trading**: ETFs trade throughout the day at fluctuating prices, like stocks. Traditional mutual funds are priced once per day, after markets close.
- **Minimum investment**: ETFs can often be bought for the price of a single share; some mutual funds require a minimum initial investment.
- **Cost**: ETFs are frequently — though not always — cheaper to own than actively managed mutual funds, since many track an index rather than paying a manager to pick investments.

## Expense ratios matter

Every ETF charges an **expense ratio** — an annual fee, expressed as a percentage of your investment, that covers the fund's operating costs. A seemingly small difference (say, 0.03% vs. 0.75%) compounds meaningfully over decades, since higher fees quietly reduce your net returns every single year.

## Types of ETFs

- **Broad market index ETFs** — track a wide index like the S&P 500 or total stock market.
- **Sector ETFs** — focus on one industry, like technology or healthcare.
- **Bond ETFs** — hold baskets of government or corporate bonds.
- **International ETFs** — provide exposure to markets outside an investor's home country.

## Key terms

- **Expense ratio**: the annual fee charged as a percentage of assets invested.
- **Index tracking**: the strategy of mirroring a benchmark's holdings rather than actively picking investments.
- **NAV (Net Asset Value)**: the per-share value of the fund's underlying holdings.
