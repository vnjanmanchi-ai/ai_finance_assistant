# Risk Tolerance Explained

Risk tolerance describes how much investment volatility — including the possibility of loss — an individual investor is willing and able to withstand in pursuit of returns. It's a personal, situational measure, not a fixed personality trait, and it shapes how a portfolio should reasonably be structured.

## Two different things people often conflate

- **Risk tolerance** is the emotional/psychological willingness to endure market ups and downs — how comfortable someone is watching their portfolio value fluctuate, especially during a downturn.
- **Risk capacity** is the practical, financial ability to absorb a loss — shaped by factors like time horizon, income stability, existing savings, and upcoming financial obligations.

Someone might feel emotionally comfortable with high risk but have low risk capacity (for example, someone nearing retirement with limited savings), or the reverse. A sound approach to investing generally accounts for both.

## Factors that typically influence risk tolerance

- **Time horizon**: money needed in 2 years generally calls for a more conservative approach than money that won't be touched for 30 years, since there's more time to recover from a downturn.
- **Financial goals**: saving for a house down payment next year carries different risk considerations than saving for retirement decades away.
- **Income stability**: steady, secure income can support a higher risk tolerance than unpredictable income.
- **Emotional response to loss**: some investors panic and sell during downturns, locking in losses; understanding this tendency in advance helps in choosing an appropriate strategy.

## Common risk tolerance categories

Financial tools often bucket investors into broad categories for simplicity:

- **Conservative** — prioritizes protecting principal, accepts lower potential returns, typically favors bonds and cash.
- **Moderate** — seeks a balance between growth and stability, typically a mix of stocks and bonds.
- **Aggressive** — prioritizes long-term growth, accepts significant short-term volatility, typically favors a higher proportion of stocks.

These categories are simplifications meant as a starting point for discussion, not a precise formula — real financial planning also weighs risk capacity, specific goals, and individual circumstances.

## Key terms

- **Volatility**: the degree to which an investment's value fluctuates over time.
- **Time horizon**: the length of time before an investor expects to need the invested money.
- **Drawdown**: the decline from a portfolio's peak value to a subsequent low point.
