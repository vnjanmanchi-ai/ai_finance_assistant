# Understanding Diversification

Diversification is the practice of spreading investments across different assets so that no single investment's poor performance can seriously damage your overall portfolio. The common shorthand — "don't put all your eggs in one basket" — captures the core idea, but the mechanics behind why it works are worth understanding.

## Why diversification works

Different asset types don't all move the same way at the same time. Stocks, bonds, real estate, and cash tend to respond differently to the same economic event — rising interest rates might hurt bond prices while having little direct effect on a particular company's stock. By holding a mix of assets whose prices don't move in lockstep (a property called **low correlation**), a decline in one part of a portfolio can be offset or cushioned by stability or gains elsewhere.

## Levels of diversification

Diversification operates on multiple layers:

1. **Across asset classes** — mixing stocks, bonds, real estate, cash.
2. **Within an asset class** — holding many different stocks across industries and company sizes, rather than concentrating in one sector or a handful of companies.
3. **Across geographies** — including international as well as domestic investments, since different economies don't always move together.

## What diversification does — and doesn't — protect against

Diversification reduces **company-specific risk** (the danger that one company's problems severely hurt your portfolio) and can smooth out volatility over time. It does not eliminate **market risk** — broad downturns that affect nearly all asset classes at once, like a widespread recession, can still reduce a well-diversified portfolio's value. Diversification is about managing risk, not guaranteeing gains or eliminating loss entirely.

## A simple way to think about it

A portfolio concentrated in a single stock might double in value — or lose most of its value — based on that one company's fortunes. A diversified portfolio smooths out those extremes: fewer dramatic wins, but also fewer dramatic losses, which is often a more sustainable way to grow wealth over long time horizons.

## Key terms

- **Correlation**: a measure of how closely two investments move in relation to each other.
- **Asset allocation**: the specific mix of asset classes (stocks, bonds, cash, etc.) held in a portfolio.
- **Concentration risk**: the risk created by having too much invested in one company, sector, or asset type.
