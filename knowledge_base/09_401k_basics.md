# 401(k) Basics

A 401(k) is an employer-sponsored retirement savings plan that lets employees contribute a portion of their paycheck to a tax-advantaged investment account, often with contributions matched, in part, by the employer.

## How contributions work

Employees elect to contribute a percentage of each paycheck, which is deducted automatically before it hits their bank account. Contributions can generally be made in two ways, depending on the plan type:

- **Traditional 401(k)**: contributions are made pre-tax, reducing taxable income in the year they're made. Withdrawals in retirement are taxed as ordinary income.
- **Roth 401(k)**: contributions are made with after-tax dollars, so there's no upfront tax break, but qualified withdrawals in retirement — including all investment growth — are tax-free.

## The employer match

Many employers match a portion of employee contributions, up to a certain percentage of salary — for example, matching 50% of contributions up to 6% of pay. This match is effectively free money added to retirement savings, and financial guidance commonly emphasizes contributing at least enough to capture the full employer match before directing money elsewhere, since leaving a match unclaimed is leaving compensation on the table.

## Contribution limits and rules

The IRS sets annual limits on how much can be contributed to a 401(k), and these limits are adjusted periodically for inflation. There are also rules around **vesting** — the schedule by which an employee gains full ownership of employer-matched funds — and penalties for withdrawing funds before retirement age, except under specific qualifying circumstances.

## Investment choices within a 401(k)

401(k) plans typically offer a curated menu of investment options — often a selection of mutual funds or target-date funds — rather than unlimited choice of individual stocks. **Target-date funds** automatically adjust their asset mix to become more conservative as the target retirement year approaches, which makes them a common default option for participants who prefer a hands-off approach.

## Why the tax treatment matters

The core value of a 401(k) beyond the employer match is tax-advantaged growth: investments grow without being taxed year to year (unlike a regular brokerage account, where dividends and realized gains can be taxed annually), which allows compounding to work more efficiently over a long time horizon.

## Key terms

- **Vesting schedule**: the timeline over which an employee gains full ownership of employer-matched contributions.
- **Target-date fund**: a fund that automatically shifts to a more conservative allocation as a specified date approaches.
- **Contribution limit**: the maximum amount the IRS allows to be contributed to a 401(k) in a given year.
