# IRA Basics: Traditional vs. Roth

An Individual Retirement Account (IRA) is a tax-advantaged account that anyone with earned income can open independently, without needing an employer to sponsor it — unlike a 401(k). The two most common types, Traditional and Roth, differ mainly in when the tax advantage applies.

## Traditional IRA

Contributions to a Traditional IRA may be tax-deductible in the year they're made, depending on income and whether the contributor (or their spouse) also has access to an employer retirement plan. The money then grows tax-deferred, and withdrawals in retirement are taxed as ordinary income. This structure is generally most beneficial for someone who expects to be in a lower tax bracket in retirement than they are during their working years.

## Roth IRA

Contributions to a Roth IRA are made with after-tax dollars — no upfront tax deduction — but qualified withdrawals in retirement, including all investment growth, are entirely tax-free. Roth IRAs also have income limits: above a certain income threshold, the ability to contribute directly phases out. A Roth IRA is generally most advantageous for someone who expects to be in the same or a higher tax bracket in retirement, or who values tax-free withdrawals for planning flexibility.

## Key differences at a glance

- **Tax treatment**: Traditional taxes withdrawals later; Roth taxes contributions now and nothing later.
- **Income limits**: Roth IRAs restrict eligibility to contribute directly above certain income levels; Traditional IRA contributions are available regardless of income, though the tax deduction may be limited.
- **Required withdrawals**: Traditional IRAs generally require minimum withdrawals starting at a certain age; Roth IRAs do not require withdrawals during the original owner's lifetime.

## Contribution limits

The IRS sets an annual contribution limit that applies across all of an individual's IRAs combined (Traditional and Roth together, not each separately), and this limit is periodically adjusted. There are also additional "catch-up" contribution allowances for older savers.

## Early withdrawal considerations

Both account types generally impose a penalty on withdrawals taken before retirement age, in addition to any taxes owed, though there are specific exceptions (such as certain first-time home purchases or education expenses) that vary by account type and are subject to IRS rules.

## Choosing between them

The decision often comes down to a comparison between current tax bracket and expected future tax bracket, though many people ultimately hold both types to create flexibility in how they draw down income in retirement.

## Key terms

- **Tax-deferred growth**: investment growth that isn't taxed until money is withdrawn.
- **Required minimum distribution (RMD)**: the minimum amount that must be withdrawn annually from certain retirement accounts starting at a specified age.
- **Catch-up contribution**: an additional contribution amount allowed for savers above a certain age.
