# How Compound Interest Works

Compound interest is interest calculated not just on the original amount of money invested (the principal), but also on the interest that money has already earned. Over time, this creates a snowball effect — your money earns returns, and then those returns start earning returns of their own.

## Simple interest vs. compound interest

With **simple interest**, you earn a return only on the original principal each period. With **compound interest**, each period's earnings are added to the principal, so the next period's return is calculated on a larger base. The more frequently interest compounds (annually, monthly, daily), and the longer the money is left to grow, the larger the difference between the two becomes.

## A concrete illustration

Consider $10,000 invested at an average 7% annual return:
- After 10 years, simple, non-compounded growth would add $7,000, reaching $17,000.
- With annual compounding, the same $10,000 grows to roughly $19,700 — because each year's gains are added to a growing base.
- Extend that same $10,000 to 30 years at 7% compounded annually, and it grows to roughly $76,000 — the growth accelerates dramatically the longer the money compounds.

## Why time matters more than almost anything else

Because compounding accelerates over time, starting early has an outsized effect compared to contributing more money later. An investor who starts contributing a decade earlier, even with smaller amounts, often ends up ahead of someone who contributes more but starts later — simply because their money had more compounding periods to grow.

## The flip side: compounding debt

The same mathematical principle works against you with debt — particularly high-interest debt like credit cards. Unpaid interest gets added to the balance, and future interest is charged on that larger balance, which is why revolving debt can grow quickly if only minimum payments are made.

## Key terms

- **Principal**: the original amount of money invested or borrowed.
- **Compounding frequency**: how often interest is calculated and added to the balance (annually, monthly, daily).
- **Rate of return**: the percentage gain (or loss) on an investment over a given period.
