# What Is a Bond?

A bond is essentially a loan. When you buy a bond, you're lending money to an issuer — a government, municipality, or corporation — which agrees to pay you back the original amount (the **principal** or **face value**) at a set future date (the **maturity date**), along with periodic interest payments (the **coupon**) along the way.

## Why bonds exist

Governments and companies issue bonds to raise money for projects, operations, or expansion without giving up ownership stakes the way selling stock would. In return for lending your money, you receive a predictable stream of interest payments.

## Main types of bonds

- **Government bonds** (e.g., U.S. Treasury bonds) — generally considered very low credit risk since they're backed by the government's ability to tax and print currency.
- **Municipal bonds** — issued by states or local governments, often with tax advantages on the interest earned.
- **Corporate bonds** — issued by companies, offering higher yields than government bonds to compensate for higher credit risk.

## How bond prices and interest rates relate

Bond prices move inversely to interest rates. When prevailing interest rates rise, existing bonds paying a lower fixed coupon become less attractive, so their market price falls. When rates fall, existing bonds with higher fixed coupons become more valuable, so their price rises. This relationship is one of the most important — and most commonly misunderstood — concepts in fixed income.

## Credit risk and ratings

Bond issuers are assigned credit ratings by agencies (such as Moody's or S&P) that estimate the likelihood the issuer will make its payments on time. Bonds rated below a certain threshold are often called "high-yield" or "junk" bonds — they pay higher interest to compensate investors for the added risk of default.

## Why investors hold bonds

Bonds are generally more stable in price than stocks and provide a predictable income stream, which is why they're often used to reduce overall portfolio volatility and provide steadier returns, especially for investors closer to needing their money.

## Key terms

- **Coupon rate**: the fixed interest rate a bond pays, expressed as a percentage of face value.
- **Yield**: the effective return an investor earns, which changes as bond prices move (distinct from the fixed coupon rate).
- **Maturity**: the date the issuer repays the bond's face value in full.
